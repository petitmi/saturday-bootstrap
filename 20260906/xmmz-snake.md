# How Snakes Eat Prey Larger Than Themselves

## Opening Hook

On September 26, 2005, researchers in Everglades National Park found something strange: a 13-foot Burmese python, dead, with its belly torn wide open — and the hindquarters of a 6-foot alligator sticking out of the wound. The snake had tried to swallow the alligator whole. Both animals ended up dead.

So how does an animal with no arms and no legs manage to eat something bigger than itself in the first place — and why does that same trick sometimes kill it? Tonight I'll walk you through the anatomy that makes this possible, how a snake actually kills its prey, and how the whole system can fail.

---

## Act 1: The Anatomy of Swallowing

Let's start with what's happening inside the snake's body when it eats.

**The lungs.** A snake has two lungs, but they're very asymmetrical. The right lung is a long, tube-like structure — in pythons and boas it typically runs somewhere around a tenth of the snake's total body length, though in some other snake families it can stretch much further, up to roughly 80 percent of body length. The left lung, by contrast, is dramatically reduced — in many species it's little more than a stub. This isn't a defect; it's an adaptation. Because one lung is so reduced, it can be pushed aside during swallowing, leaving room for prey to pass through the body cavity.

**The trachea.** Next to the lungs is the trachea, or windpipe — the same organ we have, which pulls air from outside into the body. The opening of the trachea, called the glottis, can actively extend forward and to the side of the mouth when the snake swallows something large — almost like a built-in snorkel. That means even while a snake is eating an animal far bigger than its own head, it can still breathe. The airway simply pokes out past the food instead of getting blocked by it.

**The heart.** The heart is also mobile — it shifts position to make room as a large meal moves through the body.

**The stomach and esophagus.** Both the stomach and the esophagus are highly elastic. As prey moves from the mouth, down the esophagus, and into the stomach, both organs stretch dramatically to accommodate it — far beyond what you'd expect from their resting size.

**Digestion.** So how does a snake digest something as solid as bone? The answer is stomach acid — and snake stomach acid is extraordinarily strong, strong enough to dissolve bone almost entirely. This is why snake droppings don't contain identifiable bone fragments; the acid breaks nearly everything down before it ever leaves the body.

**The jaw.** Now look at the skull. There's a bone called the quadrate bone, which connects the upper jaw to the lower jaw — and critically, it's a mobile joint, not a fixed one. This is what allows a snake to open its mouth to an extreme angle — up to around 150 degrees in some large species — wide enough to engulf prey many times the width of its own head.

**The teeth.** Finally, the teeth. Snake teeth curve backward, unlike the teeth of most animals. This isn't for cutting or chewing — it's a one-way hook. Once prey is caught between those backward-curving teeth, no amount of struggling or fighting will let it escape. Every movement the prey makes to pull away only drives it deeper in.

## Act 2: How a Snake Kills

Some snakes have fangs that inject venom, subduing prey chemically before it's ever swallowed. But many snakes rely on something simpler and, honestly, more elegant: constriction.

Here's the surprising part — and it's something scientists themselves got wrong for decades. For years, everyone assumed constriction killed by suffocation: squeeze the chest, stop the breathing. But a 2015 study on boa constrictors overturned that. Researchers measured a prey animal's blood pressure in real time during constriction — and its circulation collapsed within seconds, far too fast to be suffocation. What constriction actually does is cut off blood flow to the heart and brain. The animal loses consciousness from lack of blood flow before it would ever suffocate from lack of air.

And a snake doesn't need raw strength to do this — just precise, well-placed pressure at exactly the right moment. Think of it less like crushing and more like pinching a garden hose shut: you don't need brute force, just the right pressure in the right place. That's enough to shut down a heartbeat.

This combination — a hyper-flexible jaw, a repositionable airway, and a precision kill technique — is powerful enough that a snake can take down prey as large as an alligator.

---

## Act 3: When Eating Becomes Fatal

Which brings us back to that python in the Everglades.

Snakes are, in fact, most vulnerable to death *while eating* or shortly afterward — and there are a few distinct ways this happens.

**Miscalculation — and a fight from the inside.** Sometimes a snake simply misjudges whether it can digest something, and the meal is too large for the stomach to safely hold. But the Everglades case is even stranger than a simple overstretch: biologists who examined the remains believe the alligator may not have been fully dead when it was swallowed, and that it clawed and kicked its way through the python's stomach wall from the inside as the snake tried to digest it. So the rupture wasn't just the stomach giving out under size — it may have been the prey fighting back after it was already inside.

**Choking.** Sometimes the prey itself is the problem — thick fur or a difficult shape can lodge in the throat, cutting off the snake's airway entirely.

**Vulnerability during digestion.** And even after a successful meal, a snake with a full stomach can barely move. That makes it an easy target for predators — or even other snakes — at exactly the moment it has no way to find cover or defend itself.

So the same system that lets a snake eat something enormous is also the system most likely to kill it.

## Closing

That's the trade-off at the heart of this whole strategy: extreme flexibility unlocks extreme meals, but it comes with a real risk of catastrophic failure. The python in the Everglades is proof of exactly how high that risk can be.
