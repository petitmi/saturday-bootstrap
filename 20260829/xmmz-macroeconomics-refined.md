# Macroeconomic Dashboard — Presentation Script

## Opening: Global GDP

Let's start with the headline number — GDP as a share of global output.

Looking at this pie chart, the U.S. and China together account for roughly half of world GDP. Japan, India, and several European countries follow. Canada ranks around 10th overall.

Now let's move into the metrics I use to assess how a country's economy is actually performing.

## Layer 1: Flows — Real GDP Growth

The first and most closely watched figure is **real GDP growth** — the rate at which an economy is expanding, adjusted for inflation.

Vietnam leads with the highest real GDP growth in this set, at 6.5%. India follows closely behind. Vietnam and India both benefit from lower labor costs, which helps explain their strong growth numbers.

China has transformed from a labor-intensive economy to a more high-tech, innovation-driven one. As a result, its GDP growth has slowed somewhat, now sitting at 4.2%.

Argentina is a particularly interesting case — I'll come back to it when we look at inflation. Some of what looks like "growth" there is really about capital flows and currency dynamics rather than real output.

Singapore and Australia also show solid growth. The U.S. sits roughly mid-pack — around 25th overall. Canada comes in at 1.5%, which is actually higher than several European economies, including the UK, Germany, France, Italy, and Spain.

## Inflation (CPI)

Inflation is essentially the "noise" layered on top of GDP growth — it's not real output, just rising prices.

Argentina has an extremely high inflation rate, around 30%. Turkey follows — an interesting case since it's often grouped with European emerging markets. Nigeria also shows high inflation, though its overall GDP share is small, so I won't dwell on it.

Russia's inflation is elevated too, likely tied to the effects of sanctions. India, Brazil, South Africa, Mexico, and Vietnam all show relatively high inflation — which tracks, since inflation tends to move alongside stronger growth.

The U.S. sits at 2.9%, a bit above the commonly cited healthy benchmark of 2%. Canada is similar, at 2.8%.

China, by contrast, has inflation of just 0.8% — bordering on deflation, well below the 2% benchmark. This suggests China's economy is cooling, largely due to its shrinking real estate sector — a "soft landing" that may take several more years to fully play out. Canada is also cooling its real estate sector, but interestingly still holds relatively high inflation.

## Unemployment Rate

South Africa has a strikingly high unemployment rate, which — combined with its low 1.2% GDP growth and high inflation — points to broader economic strain.

Turkey also shows a high unemployment rate alongside its high inflation.

Focusing on the highest-inflation countries specifically: Argentina's unemployment rate is 6.5%, which is actually within the commonly cited healthy range of 4–6%.

Canada and France both show relatively high unemployment, but not alarmingly so — likely because strong social safety nets reduce the pressure on people to take any available job.

The U.S. sits at a healthy 4.6%. China's unemployment is 5.2%, which is more concerning there given its weaker social safety net compared to the U.S. or Europe.

Singapore has the lowest unemployment rate in this set, at just 2%. Vietnam (2.2%) and South Korea (2.8%) are similarly low — an interesting pattern worth digging into further.

## Current Account Balance

The current account balance reflects a nation's net income from trade — exports minus imports.

Norway shows the highest current account balance, driven largely by oil exports. Ireland follows, which fits its export-heavy economic structure. Germany and Switzerland also post high surpluses as major export economies. Vietnam and South Korea show strong current account positions too — South Korea in particular has solid metrics across the board.

The U.S. is at negative 3.7%, which reflects its consumption-driven economy — something we'll return to shortly.

## Fiscal Balance

Fiscal balance is government revenue minus government spending — what's left after taxation covers expenditures.

Norway again posts a strong positive fiscal balance, along with Ireland. Most other countries in this set run a fiscal deficit.

China shows the lowest fiscal balance, largely due to heavy government spending — including on AI — while also managing high unemployment, a cooling economy, and support for its real estate sector.

## The Five-Layer Framework

The metrics above represent one layer of a broader, five-layer framework I built for this assessment:

1. **Flows** — how much economic activity/liquidity is moving through the country (GDP growth, inflation, unemployment, etc.)
2. **Stocks** — how much accumulated wealth the country holds
3. **Structure** — how the economy is composed across agriculture, industry, and services. More developed economies typically have a higher share of services.
4. **Dynamics** — where the economy is headed: government R&D spending, demographics (population age, fertility rate, working-age population, urbanization), and CO2 intensity per unit of GDP as a measure of environmental sustainability.
5. **Vulnerabilities** — where the economy is exposed: private non-financial sector credit levels, and commodity share of exports (a high share signals greater economic fragility).

Together, these five layers give a fuller picture than GDP growth alone — showing not just how fast an economy is growing, but how resilient, balanced, and sustainable that growth is.
